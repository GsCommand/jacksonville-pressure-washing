Deck Cleaning in Jacksonville: Wood, Composite and Trex Deck Cleaning

How deck-cleaning methods change for wood, composite and Trex products in Jacksonville’s outdoor environment.

Updated August 17, 2026 · Jacksonville Pressure Washing field guide

Written and reviewed by Jacksonville Pressure Washing, a licensed and insured exterior-cleaning company serving Jacksonville and surrounding Northeast Florida communities.

Quick answer: Deck cleaning starts by identifying the product. Wood, capped composite and older composite decking can have very different manufacturer limits. A pressure setting that is acceptable for one deck can permanently mark another.

Jacksonville context: Deck cleaning in Jacksonville can involve pressure-treated wood, older coated wood and composite products with very different maintenance limits. Northeast Florida humidity, shade and rain can support organic buildup, but material and existing finish should determine the process. View Jacksonville service coverage.

Wood decks need condition-based cleaning

Wood species, age, previous stain and sun exposure affect how the surface responds. Excessive pressure can raise grain and leave lines. If the deck will be stained afterward, the cleaning method should also support the coating manufacturer’s preparation requirements.

Composite is not one universal material

Modern capped composite products can have manufacturer-approved washing limits, while older generations may carry stronger warnings against pressure washing. Brand and product line matter more than the generic word composite.

Trex publishes product-specific guidance

Trex states that some current product lines can be gently pressure washed with a fan tip within stated limits, while it does not recommend pressure washing certain earlier-generation products. That is a good example of why manufacturer instructions should be checked before cleaning.

Florida debris can support organic growth

Pollen, leaves, food residue and damp debris can collect in board texture and gaps. Regular removal of debris can reduce the material that supports mildew and mold-like growth on the surface.

What to inspect before deck cleaning

  • Product and age: Wood species and composite product generations can have different care limits.
  • Existing coating: Stain, paint or sealer may be sound, failing or partially worn.
  • Fasteners and boards: Loose boards, splinters, exposed fasteners and damaged edges deserve attention before washing.
  • Future coating plans: Cleaning before staining should match the coating manufacturer’s preparation requirements.

Why a test area matters

A small inconspicuous test can reveal raised grain, coating loss, color change or manufacturer-sensitive composite surfaces before the entire deck is cleaned. This is especially important when the deck product or previous coating is unknown. The safest process is the least aggressive method that achieves the cleaning goal without creating a new restoration problem.

The safest cleaning decision starts with the material, existing damage, coating condition, access and the type of staining present.

Protect the deck surface you want to keep

Wood can be scarred or furred by concentrated pressure, while composites may have manufacturer-specific cleaning limits and older coatings can fail unevenly. Before cleaning, inspect fasteners, splinters, loose boards, previous stain and product type. If the deck will be stained afterward, cleaning should align with the coating manufacturer’s preparation and moisture requirements.

Common questions

Can Trex be pressure washed?

Some Trex product lines allow controlled pressure washing within manufacturer limits, while older lines may not. Identify the product first.

Can wood decks be damaged by a pressure washer?

Yes. Excessive pressure can raise grain, splinter wood or leave visible cleaning marks.

Should the deck manufacturer guide the method?

Yes. Product-specific care instructions should take priority over a generic pressure setting.
